# Turning a dipole

A folded dipole is two quarter-wave rods fed in the middle: the antenna on a lath that an FM radio on the roof once had. It picks up a station best when it lies across the direction the waves come from, and not at all when it points at the station. Here the dipole feeds a tuned circuit, a germanium diode and a meter — a field-strength meter, powered by the station itself.

**Teaches:** a dipole's figure-eight pattern, broadside and end-on, why an antenna is turned for best reception

## What to do

Click **Sim** and turn the dipole's dial. Watch the meter, and probe the top of the tuned circuit.

## What you should see

- At 0° the meter reads its most: the dipole lies across the station's direction.
- At 45° less; at 90° — the dipole points at the station — nothing at all.
- At 180° the same as at 0°: the back is as good as the front.

## Why it works

The station's electric field drives current along the dipole's two halves; the voltage at its feed point is the field times its effective length (about a metre at 100 MHz) times the cosine of the angle between the dipole's broadside and the station. Pointing at the station, the field has no component along the rods and nothing is induced. The tuned circuit picks out 98 MHz; the diode rectifies its swing into a DC the meter shows.
